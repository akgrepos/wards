## Do Now
1. Make the area safe
   - If: danger to the patient, staff or others ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, aaep-beta-restraint-2012]
   - Call for help early (security or the behavioral response team). One clinician speaks to the patient, the rest stay visible and back that clinician up. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012]
   - Stay two arm-lengths away with a clear exit. Remove sharp objects, cords and loose equipment. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, common-practice]
2. Check for an immediately reversible cause
   - If: any agitated patient ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
   - Check: Fingerstick glucose, oxygen saturation, temperature, blood pressure and heart rate, plus pain, a full bladder, and alcohol or sedative use. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]

   {{dose:glucose-oral-hypoglycemia}}

   {{dose:dextrose-50-hypoglycemia}}

   - Chronic alcohol use or poor nutrition: give thiamine. Glucose need not wait for it. ^[common-practice]

   {{dose:thiamine-alcohol-withdrawal}}

3. De-escalate first
   - Introduce yourself, speak briefly and calmly, ask what the patient wants, offer food, a drink or a phone call, and set limits without threats. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012]
   - Offer oral medication as a choice. Patients accept it more often when they take part in the decision. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
4. Offer oral medication matched to the cause
   - If: de-escalation has not settled the patient and the patient will take a tablet ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
   - Check: {{calc:qtc}} and QT-prolonging drugs when an antipsychotic is chosen. Avoid benzodiazepines unless the cause is withdrawal or stimulant intoxication. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, apa-delirium-2025]

   {{dose:olanzapine-agitation-po}}

   {{dose:lorazepam-agitation-adult}}

5. Give an IM dose for imminent danger
   - If: the patient refuses oral medication or is an immediate danger after de-escalation ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, acep-severe-agitation-2023]
   - Check: Allergy, {{calc:qtc}} if available, and the cause. Avoid IM olanzapine with a parenteral benzodiazepine. ^[label-zyprexa-2026, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

   {{dose:olanzapine-agitation-im}}

   - Warning: Hypotension ^[label-zyprexa-2026]

   {{dose:haloperidol-agitation-adult}}

   - Warning: Torsades ^[label-haloperidol-inj-2026]
6. Restrain only as a last resort
   - If: medication and de-escalation have failed and there is imminent danger ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, cms-restraint-482-13]
   - Check: Give the medication before or right after restraining. Never prone. One clinician watches the airway. A physician or licensed practitioner orders it, and the patient is seen face-to-face within 1 hour. ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, cms-restraint-482-13, common-practice]
7. Reassess and escalate
   - Check: Sedation, breathing, vital signs, then the cause: a persistent medical cause or no response to two doses calls for psychiatry and medicine review. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]

:::note
- Treat agitation as a symptom: new agitation in a hospitalized adult is delirium or another medical cause until shown otherwise. See [[neuro-delirium|delirium]] and [[neuro-alcohol-withdrawal|alcohol withdrawal]]. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
:::

## At a Glance
- Look for and treat a medical cause first (hypoxemia, hypoglycemia, pain, urinary retention, withdrawal, delirium, intoxication, medication effects). Agitation is a symptom. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
- Verbal de-escalation before any drug. Offer oral medication and involve the patient in the choice. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, acep-severe-agitation-2023]
- Match the drug to the cause: a benzodiazepine for alcohol or sedative withdrawal and stimulant intoxication, an antipsychotic for psychosis and delirium, and avoid benzodiazepines in delirium from other causes. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
- Cautions before an antipsychotic: {{calc:qtc}}, parkinsonism, dementia boxed warning, older age. Avoid IM olanzapine with a parenteral benzodiazepine. ^[label-zyprexa-2026, label-haloperidol-inj-2026, aha-accf-qt-2010]
- Restraint is the last resort for imminent danger: give medication with it, never prone, monitor closely, release as early as possible. ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, cms-restraint-482-13, common-practice]
- Call psychiatry for a psychiatric cause, a suicide or violence risk, an involuntary hold question, or agitation that persists after two doses. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]

## Definition and Staging
- Agitation ranges from restlessness to combativeness. Rate it with the {{calc:rass}} on the ward (positive scores +1 to +4) or the {{calc:bars}}, and re-rate after each intervention. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, padis-2018]

:::scale Agitation level (positive scores on the Richmond scale)
- Restless (+1): anxious or apprehensive, movements not aggressive. De-escalate and treat the cause. ^[padis-2018, aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, common-practice]
- Agitated (+2): frequent purposeless movement. Oral medication if de-escalation fails. ^[padis-2018, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]
- Very agitated (+3): pulls or removes tubes or catheters, aggressive. Offer oral, or IM if refused. ^[padis-2018, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]
- Combative (+4): overtly combative or violent, immediate danger to staff. IM medication, restraint only if needed. ^[padis-2018, aaep-beta-restraint-2012, common-practice]
:::

- Sedation goal after treatment: calm and arousable (the patient can cooperate with evaluation), not asleep. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

## First Steps
1. Safety
   - Call for help early. Do not enter a room with a patient who has a weapon. One speaker. Keep a clear exit. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, common-practice]
   - Remove hazards and lines that are not needed. A sitter or one-to-one observation for ongoing risk. ^[common-practice]
2. Vital signs and glucose now
   - Vital signs, oxygen saturation, temperature and fingerstick glucose. Abnormalities are treated before or while the patient is calmed. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
   - Hypoxemia, hypoglycemia, shock, hyperthermia and a seizure or postictal state are immediate threats. See [[crit-hypoxemic-respiratory-failure]]. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
3. Pain, bladder, bowel
   - Treat pain. Relieve urinary retention (bladder scan, catheter). Check for constipation or impaction. ^[common-practice]
4. Review medicines and substances
   - New or stopped drugs: opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, corticosteroids, antiepileptics, dopamine agonists, stimulants, serotonergic drugs. Also ask about alcohol, sedative or opioid withdrawal and intoxication. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
5. Decide capacity and consent
   - Involuntary medication or restraint only for imminent danger to the patient or others. Otherwise a patient with capacity may refuse. State law and hospital policy govern the details. ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, cms-restraint-482-13, common-practice]
6. Code status and who to call
   - Note code status, surrogate and family contact. Call psychiatry early if a psychiatric cause is likely. ^[common-practice]

## Causes
- **Hypoxemia and hypercapnia:** [!cannot-miss] pneumonia, COPD exacerbation, pulmonary embolism, heart failure, opioid or sedative respiratory depression. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- **Metabolic:** [!cannot-miss] hypoglycemia, hyponatremia ([[renal-hyponatremia|hyponatremia]]), hypercalcemia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypo- or hyperthyroidism, hyperglycemia. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- **Infection and inflammation:** [!cannot-miss] sepsis ([[crit-sepsis|sepsis]]), urinary tract infection ([[id-complicated-uti|complicated UTI]]), meningitis or encephalitis. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- **Delirium:** [!most-common] hyperactive or mixed delirium from any of these causes in hospitalized older adults. See [[neuro-delirium|delirium]]. ^[apa-delirium-2025, nice-delirium-2023]
- **Withdrawal:** [!cannot-miss] alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal (see [[neuro-alcohol-withdrawal|alcohol withdrawal]]), opioid withdrawal, nicotine withdrawal. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, asam-alcohol-withdrawal-2020]
- **Intoxication:** stimulants (cocaine, methamphetamine), synthetic cannabinoids, anticholinergics, alcohol and sedatives, dissociatives. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- **Medication effects:** corticosteroids, anticholinergics, benzodiazepines (paradoxical), opioids, antiepileptics, dopamine agonists, serotonergic drugs, antipsychotic akathisia. ^[ags-beers-2023, aaep-beta-medical-2012]
- **Neurologic:** [!cannot-miss] stroke, intracranial hemorrhage or head injury, seizure or postictal state, nonconvulsive status epilepticus, Wernicke encephalopathy, dementia with behavioral symptoms. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- **Physical discomfort:** [!most-common] pain, urinary retention, constipation, hunger or thirst, sleep deprivation, restraints or lines. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
- **Psychiatric:** psychosis, mania, agitated depression, anxiety or panic, personality disorder, catatonia (excited form), posttraumatic stress. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]

## Presentation
- Ask staff and family for the baseline and onset: sudden change in hours to days with fluctuation suggests delirium. Known psychiatric illness with a gradual build suggests a psychiatric cause. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
- Predictors of violence: recent violence or threats, clenched fists, loud speech, pacing, intrusiveness, substance use, a history of violence or psychosis. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]
- Medical clues: abnormal vital signs, disorientation or inattention, fluctuating course, visual hallucinations (delirium, withdrawal), tremor and sweating (withdrawal), pinpoint or dilated pupils, focal deficits, fever, trauma. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- Psychiatric clues: auditory hallucinations or paranoia with a clear sensorium, pressured speech and decreased need for sleep (mania), known illness. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- Do not attribute agitation to a psychiatric cause until medical causes are considered: every new agitation in a hospitalized patient is a medical problem first. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]

## Diagnosis
#### Bedside
- Vital signs, oxygen saturation, temperature, fingerstick glucose, and a bladder scan if retention is possible, in every agitated patient. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- Physical examination, with a neurologic examination, signs of trauma, and signs of withdrawal or intoxication. Add collateral history and a medication review. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
- Delirium screen when attention can be tested: {{calc:4at}}. See [[neuro-delirium|delirium]]. ^[nice-delirium-2023]

#### Tests by clue
- Common first set: BMP with calcium, magnesium, phosphate, CBC, ECG with {{calc:qtc}}, urinalysis if symptoms or signs suggest infection, blood gas if breathing is abnormal. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- Add as indicated: blood cultures, chest radiograph, liver panel, ammonia, thyroid studies, drug levels, urine toxicology (it rarely changes acute management), serum ethanol, creatine kinase. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]
- Head CT, lumbar puncture or EEG for focal findings, head injury, fever with meningismus, seizure concern, or no cause found. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, common-practice]

#### Avoid
- Delaying vital signs and glucose, or sedating before a medical cause is excluded in a patient who is cooperative. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
- Relying on urine toxicology to decide the first-line drug. ^[common-practice]

## Treatment
1. Treat the cause
   - Hypoxemia, hypoglycemia, pain, retention, infection, withdrawal and metabolic derangement: treat as in their own cards. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
   - Hypoglycemia: oral glucose if the patient can swallow, IV dextrose if not. ^[common-practice]

   {{dose:glucose-oral-hypoglycemia}}

   {{dose:dextrose-50-hypoglycemia}}

   - Chronic alcohol use or poor nutrition: give thiamine, and do not delay glucose for it. ^[common-practice]

   {{dose:thiamine-alcohol-withdrawal}}

   - Stop or reduce deliriogenic drugs. ^[ags-beers-2023, apa-delirium-2025]
2. De-escalate
   - Single speaker. Calm, brief, simple language. Ask what the patient wants. Offer food, drink, pain relief and a choice of medicine or route. Set limits without threats. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012]
   - Reduce stimulation: quiet room, lights, glasses and hearing aids, family or a sitter. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
3. Choose medication by cause and route
   - Medication follows de-escalation, or comes first when the patient is an immediate danger or the cause is life-threatening. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, acep-severe-agitation-2023]
   - Offer oral first (tablet or orally disintegrating tablet). IM if the patient refuses or the danger is imminent. Pathway: oral versus IM by cause, in the Treatment pathway on this card. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
   - Lowest effective dose. Wait for each dose to work before repeating. Reduce doses in older adults and frail patients. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
   - Before an antipsychotic: {{calc:qtc}} if possible, potassium, magnesium, QT-prolonging drugs reviewed. ^[aha-accf-qt-2010, label-haloperidol-inj-2026]
4. Withdrawal or stimulant intoxication
   - A benzodiazepine. For alcohol withdrawal, dose by the {{calc:ciwa-ar}} as in [[neuro-alcohol-withdrawal|alcohol withdrawal]]. Give thiamine. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, asam-alcohol-withdrawal-2020]
   - Undifferentiated agitation without psychosis, once delirium is unlikely: a benzodiazepine, as for withdrawal. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
   - Alcohol or other sedative intoxication: avoid benzodiazepines (added respiratory depression). An antipsychotic is preferred. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

   {{dose:lorazepam-agitation-adult}}

   {{dose:lorazepam-agitation-older}}

5. Delirium (not withdrawal)
   - Nonpharmacologic care first. An antipsychotic only for severe distress or danger, lowest dose, shortest time. Avoid benzodiazepines. ^[apa-delirium-2025, nice-delirium-2023]

   {{dose:haloperidol-delirium-low}}

   - See [[neuro-delirium|delirium]] for the full approach. ^[apa-delirium-2025]
6. Psychosis, mania, or undifferentiated agitation with psychotic features
   - An oral second-generation antipsychotic first. IM olanzapine or ziprasidone if refused. If one dose is not enough, add lorazepam rather than more antipsychotic. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, acep-severe-agitation-2023]

   {{dose:olanzapine-agitation-po}}

   {{dose:olanzapine-agitation-im}}

   - IM olanzapine with a parenteral benzodiazepine: not recommended by the label (excess sedation, cardiorespiratory depression) and avoided by the consensus statement. ^[label-zyprexa-2026, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
   - Haloperidol alone or with lorazepam is effective. It causes more extrapyramidal effects than the other choices, and added lorazepam lowers that rate. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

   {{dose:haloperidol-agitation-adult}}

   - Ziprasidone IM only with a normal {{calc:qtc}} and no cardiac disease. ^[label-geodon-im-2026, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

   {{dose:ziprasidone-agitation-im}}

7. Severe or refractory agitation (monitored setting)
   - ACEP: for faster, more effective control in the emergency department use droperidol with midazolam or an atypical antipsychotic with midazolam. A single drug should be droperidol or an atypical antipsychotic rather than midazolam alone. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023]
   - A benzodiazepine IM with continuous oxygen saturation monitoring and airway support. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023, label-midazolam-inj-2026]

   {{dose:midazolam-agitation-im}}

   - Ketamine (ACEP consensus, when safety is a concern) needs immediate hemodynamic monitoring and advanced airway management. Droperidol carries a boxed QT warning. Involve the ICU or anesthesia rather than ordering either on a ward. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023, common-practice]
8. Restraints (last resort)
   - Only for imminent danger to the patient or others when less restrictive measures have failed. The least restrictive method, for the shortest time. ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, cms-restraint-482-13]
   - Give a calming medication before or right after. Never restrain prone. Use a team of trained staff with one person protecting the head and airway. ^[cms-restraint-482-13, common-practice]
   - A physician or licensed practitioner orders it (never a standing or as-needed order). For violent or self-destructive behavior, a face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour (physician, licensed practitioner or trained registered nurse) and each order lasts at most 4 hours for adults, renewable up to 24 hours, after which the patient is seen again before a new order. ^[cms-restraint-482-13]
   - Monitoring by trained staff at intervals set by hospital policy (continuous when restraint and seclusion are combined). Check vital signs, circulation, skin, hydration and toileting. Remove restraints at the earliest possible time. ^[cms-restraint-482-13, common-practice]
9. Adverse effects
   - Acute dystonia (torticollis, oculogyric crisis, trismus) after an antipsychotic: benztropine. Laryngeal dystonia is an airway emergency. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, label-benztropine-inj-2024]

   {{dose:benztropine-agitation-dystonia}}

   - Akathisia can look like worsening agitation: review the recent antipsychotic dose before giving more. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

## Monitor
:::times{after=olanzapine-agitation-im check="Sedation, breathing, vital signs" unit=min} Reassess after an IM dose, minutes ^[common-practice, label-zyprexa-2026]
- 15
- 30
- 60
:::

- After each dose: sedation, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure and heart rate at set intervals (for example 15, 30 and 60 minutes after an IM dose), and re-rate with the {{calc:rass}}. Check for orthostatic hypotension before another IM olanzapine dose. ^[common-practice, label-zyprexa-2026]
- Repeat the ECG with {{calc:qtc}} after antipsychotic doses in patients at risk. Stop or switch for {{calc:qtc}} >500 ms or a rise of ≥60 ms. ^[aha-accf-qt-2010, label-haloperidol-inj-2026]
- Do not repeat a dose before it can act. Stacked doses and combined drugs cause oversedation and respiratory depression. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]
- In restraints: monitoring per hospital policy and the CMS limits (1-hour face-to-face, 4-hour adult orders, new assessment after 24 hours). ^[cms-restraint-482-13]
- Reassess the cause: agitation that returns or does not settle means the cause was not treated. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
- Debrief the patient and staff after a restraint or involuntary medication. Restore the therapeutic relationship. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, aaep-beta-restraint-2012]
- Review and stop as-needed antipsychotic and benzodiazepine orders daily. ^[apa-delirium-2025, ags-beers-2023]

## Escalation
- Psychiatry: a likely psychiatric cause, suicide or violence risk, an involuntary hold or capacity question, agitation not controlled after two doses, catatonia, or a patient who needs psychiatric admission. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, common-practice]
- Rapid response or ICU: airway risk, hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability, severe hyperthermia, heavy sedation, or agitation that needs ketamine or deeper sedation. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023, common-practice]
- Medicine or neurology: unexplained agitation with a focal deficit, seizure or fever. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
- Security or the behavioral response team: any danger to staff. Hospital policy and state law govern holds and restraint. ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, cms-restraint-482-13]
- Ethics, risk management or legal: the patient refuses and the danger is not imminent, or the surrogate disagrees. ^[common-practice]

## More Information
### Where Guidelines Differ: First-line medication
:::compare Parenteral medication for severe agitation
- ACEP 2023 (ED, adults 65 and younger): droperidol or an atypical antipsychotic plus midazolam. Haloperidol alone or with lorazepam is also effective. Ketamine when safety is a concern (consensus). ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023]
- AAEP 2012: choose by cause. A benzodiazepine for withdrawal, stimulant intoxication or undifferentiated agitation without psychosis. An antipsychotic for psychosis, delirium or alcohol intoxication. Avoid IM olanzapine with a benzodiazepine. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
- APA delirium 2025: low-dose antipsychotic only for severe distress or danger. Benzodiazepines only for withdrawal. ^[apa-delirium-2025]
:::

### Special Populations: Older adults, dementia, parkinsonism
- Older adults and dementia: lower doses. Antipsychotics carry a boxed warning for increased mortality in dementia-related psychosis. Beers lists them and benzodiazepines among drugs to avoid. ^[label-haloperidol-inj-2026, ags-beers-2023]
- ACEP made no recommendation for patients above 65. Delirium guidance applies. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023, apa-delirium-2025]
- Long QT: avoid ziprasidone and use haloperidol with caution. Correct potassium and magnesium. Olanzapine has less QT effect but is not risk-free. ^[aha-accf-qt-2010, label-geodon-im-2026, common-practice]
- Parkinson disease and dementia with Lewy bodies: haloperidol is contraindicated. ^[label-haloperidol-inj-2026]
- Opioids or other sedatives on board: parenteral benzodiazepines raise the risk of respiratory depression. ^[label-ativan-inj-2026, label-midazolam-inj-2026]

### Complications: Restraint and sedation
- Injury, asphyxia, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis, pressure injury, nerve injury, psychological trauma. Stimulant intoxication, obesity and heavy sedation raise the risk of sudden death in restraints. ^[aaep-beta-restraint-2012, common-practice]

### After the Acute Phase: Debrief and plan
- Explain what happened and why. Record the evaluation, de-escalation attempts and the indication. Review orders. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012, cms-restraint-482-13]

### Evidence: Key sources
- ACEP 2023: one critical question. No Level A recommendations. Evidence of 3 class II and 14 class III studies. ^[acep-severe-agitation-2023]
- The AAEP Project BETA consensus statements (2012) are expert consensus, not trials. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]

## Pearls
- Agitation is a symptom: vital signs and glucose before any drug. ^[aaep-beta-medical-2012]
- De-escalation works more often than people expect. Give the patient a choice. ^[aaep-beta-deescalation-2012]
- Lorazepam calms withdrawal but can worsen delirium. Low-potency (phenothiazine) antipsychotics lower the seizure threshold, a concern in withdrawal. ^[aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012, apa-delirium-2025]
- On the ward, avoid IM olanzapine with a parenteral benzodiazepine. ^[label-zyprexa-2026, aaep-beta-psychopharm-2012]
- Never restrain prone. Medicate with restraints. Face-to-face evaluation within the hour. ^[cms-restraint-482-13, common-practice]
